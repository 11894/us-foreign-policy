The argument in three claims

  1. Stable goals, shifting method. Security, advantage and primacy hold from the Monroe Doctrine to now, while the method swings between building institutions and acting alone.
  2. Trump’s second term is the sharpest swing since 1945. Hemispheric primacy, tariffs, bargaining through a small circle, and burden-shifting onto allies, run like a business. Courts and Congress change the tools, not the direction.
  3. China: managed rivalry. Engagement gave way to competition, and summits and truces now sit on top of continuing export controls and a Taiwan question that no one has settled.

Five eras of American foreign policy

  • 1776–1898
    Insulation and expansion

    Washington’s Farewell Address (1796) and the Monroe Doctrine (1823) put the hemisphere first.

  • 1898–1945
    Reluctant power

    The Open Door notes, the Senate’s rejection of Versailles, then Lend-Lease and Pearl Harbor.

  • 1945–1991
    Containment and the liberal order

    The UN, Bretton Woods, the Marshall Plan and NATO.

  • 1991–2016
    Primacy

    The Gulf War, Iraq and the rebalance to Asia.

  • 2017–now
    Competition and America First

    Two Trump terms and one Biden term, with China as the main competitor.

Three things stay constant across the eras: the hemisphere comes first, no rival may dominate a key region, and Washington acts without international permission when it judges its core interests at stake.

The essay

Continuity and change in United States foreign policy

The Trump administration, its predecessors and the management of China

Every American president since the Second World War has presented his foreign policy as a break with the last one, and most have kept more of it than they admitted. This essay asks how true that is today. It argues that the aims of United States foreign policy have stayed remarkably steady while the methods have swung hard, that Donald Trump’s second term is the sharpest swing since 1945, run like a business and decided by a small circle around the president, and that China, the hardest test of the argument, has ended up in managed rivalry rather than in a settlement or a collapse. It takes the country’s facts, current policy, decision-making, history, theory, political orientation and domestic politics in turn.

The country in question is a federal presidential republic of fifty states, with its capital in Washington, DC, and about 342 million people (Fortune, 2026b). It has the world’s largest economy, a nominal GDP of about $32.4 trillion in 2026 against about $20.8 trillion for China (Statista, 2026), and it funds its military at roughly $1 trillion a year, with $1.5 trillion requested for FY2027 (Mondaq, 2026). It holds a permanent seat on the UN Security Council and founded NATO, though in January 2026 it left 66 international bodies (Ward and Volcovici, 2026). Donald Trump is in his second term with JD Vance as vice president; Marco Rubio is both Secretary of State and national security adviser, and Pete Hegseth runs Defence (Colorado Politics, 2026). Size matters for the argument, because it gives Washington a freedom of choice that smaller states do not have.

Current policy starts from the National Security Strategy of 4 December 2025, which rejects the pursuit of global hegemony in favour of “America First” realism and restates the Monroe Doctrine as a “Trump Corollary” (Al Jazeera Centre for Studies, 2025; Gateway House, 2025). The strategy has been applied by force and run like a business. American forces captured Venezuela’s president on 3 January 2026 (Breaking Defense, 2026), strikes on Iran began on 28 February (Geo News, 2026), and the government now takes a 25 per cent share of Nvidia chip sales to China (ArentFox Schiff, 2026). Trump has said that a flip of the pen on tariffs brings money into the Treasury (ROIC, 2026b). Allies feel it. Trump threatened in April to leave NATO (PBS NewsHour, 2026), although a 2023 law bars withdrawal without a two-thirds Senate vote or an act of Congress, which he disputes (Semafor, 2026b), and the treaties still stand, so partners are better described as strained than turned into enemies. Ukraine felt the pressure first, when Trump paused all military aid and intelligence sharing after the Oval Office clash of 28 February 2025 (Washington Times, 2025), and by July he had sidelined Israel’s prime minister to seek a deal with Tehran (TimesLIVE, 2026). Hudson (2014) would group the causes into six determinants: power and geography, economic interests, institutions, ideology, domestic politics, and the leader with his small circle. Power and geography explain what stays constant, and the other five explain why administrations differ. Each is visible in the record: geography in the hemispheric priority, economic interest in the chip share, institutions in the Supreme Court ruling, ideology in the exit from 66 bodies, domestic politics in the visa bans, and the leader’s circle in the Venezuela and Iran decisions.

The way these decisions are made has changed as much as the decisions themselves. A policy cycle has six stages: agenda setting, information, options, decision, implementation, and oversight with feedback (Hudson, 2014). In the second Trump term the early stages are compressed. More than half of the National Security Council’s staff were dismissed, Rubio holds two senior posts at once (only Kissinger did so before him), and a small circle of Vance, Witkoff, Kushner and the chief of staff shapes policy on Ukraine, Gaza and Iran (The Majalla, 2026; Colorado Politics, 2026). With less testing of options at the start, the last stage does more of the work, and Congress learned of the July resumption of strikes on Iran by letter after they had begun (IANS, 2026). Tariffs show how. Duties on China reached 145 per cent in April 2025 (Great Lakes Customs Law, 2026) and were imposed by executive order under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. The Supreme Court ruled six to three on 20 February 2026 that the Act does not authorise tariffs, and within hours the administration moved to Section 122 and then to Sections 232 and 301 (United States Studies Centre, 2026). The chip decision took the same path, a presidential announcement first and a Bureau of Industry and Security rule afterwards (BIS, 2026).

None of this makes sense without the history, and the history is hard to tell because 45 people have held the presidency across 47 presidencies, from Roosevelt’s twelve years to Harrison’s thirty-one days. Five eras bring order. Until 1898 the country expanded and acted alone, and the Monroe Doctrine of 1823 put the hemisphere first (Mead, 2001). Until 1945 it was a reluctant power, and the Senate’s rejection of the Versailles settlement showed how far domestic politics could veto leadership. Until 1991 it built the United Nations, the Bretton Woods system and NATO and pursued containment (Gaddis, 2005), an order that lasted because Washington accepted limits on its own freedom (Ikenberry, 2011). The years to 2016 brought primacy, the Iraq war and the rebalance to Asia. Since 2017 the country has been defined by competition with China and “America First”. Three things never changed: the hemisphere comes first, no rival may dominate a key region, and Washington acts without international permission when it judges its core interests to be at stake. The method is what moved.

The four most recent administrations show the swing in miniature. Obama ended the Iraq combat mission, concluded the Iran nuclear agreement and announced a rebalance to Asia (Clinton, 2011; Bader, 2012), working through institutions and partners. Trump’s first term withdrew from the Trans-Pacific Partnership and the Iran deal, treated alliances as bargains to be repriced and made tariffs a routine instrument (White House, 2017). Biden rebuilt alliances through AUKUS and the Quad, backed Ukraine without American troops, and used the CHIPS Act and export controls as foreign policy tools (White House, 2022). Trump’s second term then reversed the alliance-first method, as the paragraphs above show. The comparison supports the central claim: the four differ in method, in how far they trust institutions and in the weight they give to values, and they agree on primacy and on China as the main competitor.

China shows the pattern clearly. Engagement ran from Nixon’s visit in 1972 to China’s entry to the World Trade Organization in 2001, on the bet that trade would liberalise Beijing, and Campbell and Ratner (2018) argue that the bet failed. The 2017 strategy named China a competitor, and Biden’s 2022 strategy called it the only rival able to reshape the order (White House, 2017; White House, 2022). The second Trump term has paired tariffs that peaked at 145 per cent with summits at Busan, Beijing and Washington that produced a truce to 10 January 2027 and no structural settlement. Each side holds a chokepoint, which is why neither can afford a full break: China supplies about 91 per cent of refined rare earths, while the United States consumes about a quarter of the world’s chips and makes about a tenth of what it uses (Klemencic and Chamberlain, 2026). Taiwan remains the sharpest issue: Washington approved about $11 billion of arms in December 2025 (Associated Press, 2026), yet Taiwan appeared in neither side’s list of deliverables at the September summit (IEU Monitoring, 2026). Three readings compete. Structural rivalry (Mearsheimer, 2001; Allison, 2017) expects truces only to delay a contest, managed interdependence expects both sides to prefer stability, and personalised bargaining expects volatility. The evidence supports each at a different level, which is why managed rivalry is the fairest label.

Theory explains different parts of this record. Realism expects a dominant power to seek regional hegemony and block rivals (Mearsheimer, 2001), which fits the hemispheric priority and the rivalry with China, and offshore balancing (Mearsheimer and Walt, 2016) matches the push to make allies carry their own regions. Liberalism holds that institutions lower the cost of cooperation (Keohane, 1984) and that the postwar order worked because the United States bound itself to rules (Ikenberry, 2011), so leaving 66 bodies erodes the order that gave its leadership legitimacy. Constructivism says interests rest on identity (Wendt, 1999), which helps explain why “America First” persuades voters. Mead’s (2001) four traditions make the same point historically: the present mix is Jacksonian and Hamiltonian, with Wilsonian values weakest. Foreign policy analysis then sorts the explanations by level, so that the system explains the constants, the state the swings and the individual the style (Hudson, 2014). Allison’s (1971) three models fit one set of facts: the rational-actor model matches the stated doctrine, the organisational-process model matches how agencies carry out a presidential decision, and the bureaucratic-politics model matches the small circle competing for the president’s ear.

Political orientation shapes the choice of instruments. The current administration draws on nationalist conservatism, sovereigntist and wary of multilateral commitments, and it shows in the exit from 66 bodies, a travel ban on 39 countries (Ahuja, 2025), tariffs used as a general tool, aid replaced by co-funded compacts and a near absence of human rights from presidential diplomacy. Obama and Biden worked from liberal internationalism, with alliances, the Paris Agreement and the Iran deal, and democracy promotion (Ikenberry, 2011). Orientation does not settle everything, though. Both strategies name China as the main competitor (White House, 2017; White House, 2022), so primacy and the contest with China survived a change of party, and the presidential system added a check of its own when the Supreme Court overturned the tariffs. Orientation shapes means more than ends.

Domestic and foreign policy feed each other, which is Putnam’s (1988) point about two-level games: a president bargains abroad and at home at once. Immigration politics produced the travel ban (Ahuja, 2025) and a separate pause on immigrant visas for 75 countries from 21 January 2026 (EIG Law, 2026), tariffs are sold as revenue, and the midterm elections on 3 November shape what the White House can promise Beijing. The flow runs back as well. American inflation rose during the Iran war and the Hormuz blockade (Al Jazeera, 2026), and the $1.5 trillion defence request would, by one budget watchdog’s estimate, add about $6.9 trillion to the national debt over ten years (Daily Herald, 2026). Congress and the courts add a constraint, changing which tool is used more than which goal is pursued. The business logic can be read as an attempt to make foreign policy pay at home, which raises the domestic stakes of every setback abroad.

A small state sees these shifts at close range. Before the cuts, USAID gave Malawi more than $350 million a year, and the Millennium Challenge Corporation’s $350 million compact has since collapsed (Kateta, 2026). Malawi has now signed a five-year, $936 million health compact in which Washington pays up to $792 million and Malawi commits an extra $143 million of its own (Kateta, 2026), and it sits on the partial visa-ban list, which suspends student and exchange visas along with immigrant and visitor visas (Ahuja, 2025). China offers another route, with zero tariffs for 53 African states from 1 May 2026 (Capital FM, 2026a). Keohane (1969) argues that small states cannot change the system but can use great-power rivalry to widen their room, and the evidence fits: access has become the currency. Malawi gains by turning Chinese market access into exports, by treating the American compact as a fiscal commitment, and by keeping control of the data terms in its health agreements. Those terms matter because the compact comes with a seven-year specimen-sharing agreement under which Malawi shares pathogen specimens and genetic data with the United States, which may pass them to up to ten outside entities (US and Malawi Governments, 2026). Washington calls this health security, and critics call it aid in exchange for data (Africanews, 2026). Narrative can drive policy too: Trump’s claim that white South Africans face a genocide, which South Africa’s government and independent experts reject, led the United States to boycott the Johannesburg G20 (Al Jazeera, 2025a).

United States foreign policy has chased the same ends for two centuries: a secure hemisphere, open markets on good terms, and no rival in command of a key region. What has changed is the method, and in 2026 the method is business, run through a small circle and corrected from outside. On China that has produced managed rivalry. For a small state such as Malawi, aid has turned into access, and the safest course is to stay useful to both powers and dependent on neither.

Further reading

Books for going deeper. These were not cited in the essay. The one-line notes say why each is useful.
  • Kissinger, H. (1994) Diplomacy. New York: Simon & Schuster.

    A long history of great-power statecraft, useful for seeing American policy as one tradition among several.

  • Kissinger, H. (2011) On China. New York: Penguin Press.

    The history of the US–China relationship from a participant’s point of view, including the Taiwan framework.

  • Rudd, K. (2022) The avoidable war: the dangers of a catastrophic conflict between the US and Xi Jinping’s China. New York: PublicAffairs.

    Sets out how both sides could manage rivalry without war.

  • Doshi, R. (2021) The long game: China’s grand strategy to displace American order. New York: Oxford University Press.

    Reads Chinese strategy as a long campaign, a useful partner to the American side of the story.

  • Kagan, R. (2006) Dangerous nation: America’s place in the world from its earliest days to the dawn of the twentieth century. New York: Knopf.

    Argues that American expansion and ambition began long before 1898.

  • Kennan, G.F. (1951) American diplomacy, 1900–1950. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.

    A realist critique of moralism in American foreign policy.

  • Brzezinski, Z. (1997) The grand chessboard: American primacy and its geostrategic imperatives. New York: Basic Books.

    A statement of the primacy logic that later strategies inherit.

  • Nye, J.S. (2004) Soft power: the means to success in world politics. New York: PublicAffairs.

    Explains influence through attraction, a useful contrast with tariffs and force.

  • Mearsheimer, J.J. (2018) The great delusion: liberal dreams and international realities. New Haven: Yale University Press.

    The realist case against liberal hegemony.

  • Walt, S.M. (2018) The hell of good intentions: America’s foreign policy elite and the decline of U.S. primacy. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

    Criticises the bipartisan consensus on primacy.

  • Brautigam, D. (2009) The dragon’s gift: the real story of China in Africa. Oxford: Oxford University Press.
